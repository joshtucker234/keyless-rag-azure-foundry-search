### S02 · Which components decide and enforce whether a session is allowed in a zero trust architecture?

- **Expect:** The Policy Decision Point (Policy Engine plus Policy Administrator) decides; the Policy Enforcement Point enables or terminates the connection.
- **Expected source:** 800-207: p.14, 18, 19, 21, 22, 23, 26, 30, 31, 37, ...
- **Got:** In a zero trust architecture, the components responsible for deciding and enforcing whether a session is allowed are:

- The Policy Engine (PE): This component makes the ultimate decision to grant, deny, or revoke access to a resource for a given subject based on enterprise policy and inputs from external sources. It logs the decision as approved or denied [1].

- The Policy Administrator (PA): This component executes the decision made by the policy engine by establishing or shutting down the communication path between the subject and the resource. It generates session-specific authentication tokens or credentials and communicates with the Policy Enforcement Point to enforce the decision [1][5].

- The Policy Enforcement Point (PEP): This system enables, monitors, and terminates connections between the subject and the enterprise resource. It forwards requests and receives policy updates from the PA, acting as the gatekeeper for communication paths [5].

Thus, the Policy Engine decides on access, and the Policy Administrator and Policy Enforcement Point enforce the session allowance in zero trust architecture.

### S08 · How long can a system be down before the mission suffers?
*Type: paraphrase*  ·  **Result in this run: ✅ pass**

- **Expect:** Maximum Tolerable Downtime (MTD): the longest a system can be unavailable before causing unacceptable mission impact, determined in the business impact analysis.
- **Expected source:** 800-34r1: p.31, 121, 144, 147
- **Got:** The maximum amount of time a system can be down before the mission suffers is defined by the Maximum Tolerable Downtime (MTD). The MTD represents the total time the system owner or authorizing official is willing to accept for a mission/business process outage or disruption, considering all impact factors. It guides the selection of recovery methods and the detail required in recovery procedures [1][3].

Additionally, the Recovery Time Objective (RTO) defines the maximum time a system resource can remain unavailable before causing unacceptable impact on other system resources, mission/business processes, and the MTD. The RTO is important for selecting technologies to meet the MTD [1][3].

For example, a mission/business process like paying vendor invoices might have an MTD of 72 hours and an RTO of 48 hours, indicating the system can be down up to 72 hours before the mission suffers unacceptable impact [3].
